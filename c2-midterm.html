Part 1

Landmark Supreme Court Cases & Reasonable Expectation of Privacy (REP)

Katz v. United States & Public REP

Items #3 & #8 Case Law
  • Principles of Katz v. US (#8):
    • Reasonable Expectation of Privacy (REP) was established.
    • You need a search warrant to go into somewhere someone has a REP.
    • Shifted Fourth Amendment protection from PLACE to PEOPLE ("The Fourth Amendment protects people, not places").
  • Two Subjects Talking in a Public Place (#3):
    • They have NO Reasonable Expectation of Privacy (No REP). What a person knowingly exposes to the public is not protected by the 4th Amendment.

Aguilar v. Texas vs. Illinois v. Gates

Items #11 & #25 Informants & PC
Aguilar v. Texas — Two-Prong Test (#25)

Established the strict two-prong test for evaluating informant tips:

  1. Basis of Knowledge (how the informant got the info)
  2. Veracity (truthfulness/credibility of the informant or reliability of the info)
Illinois v. Gates — Totality of the Circumstances (#11)

Replaced the rigid two-prong test from Aguilar with the Totality of the Circumstances test when determining Probable Cause.

Terry v. Ohio vs. Florida v. Royer

Items #12 & #24 Detention vs. Arrest
Terry v. Ohio (#12)

They are NOT under arrest as soon as you approach them to detain them. Approaching and briefly detaining a person on Reasonable Articulable Suspicion (RAS) is an investigative stop, not an arrest.

Florida v. Royer (#24)

Subject was held with no sense he was free to go and officers seized his personal artifacts (kept his airline ticket, driver's license, and luggage and moved him to a small room) — turning an investigative stop into an unlawful arrest without probable cause.

Graham v. Connor — Excessive Force Factors

Item #22 Use of Force

Graham v. Connor (#22): Evaluates excessive force under the 4th Amendment Objective Reasonableness standard using 3 core factors:

  1. Severity of the crime at issue
  2. Evading (whether the suspect is actively resisting arrest or attempting to evade arrest by flight)
  3. Danger to police or others (whether the suspect poses an immediate threat to the safety of officers or others)
Part 2

Probable Cause, Search Warrants, Standing, Exclusionary Rule & Discovery

Probable Cause Definition & Core Rules

Items #5, #6, #18, #23
Official Definition of Probable Cause (#5)

Facts and circumstances within the officer's knowledge (and of which the officer has reasonably trustworthy information) that are sufficient in themselves to warrant a person of reasonable caution in the belief that an offense has been or is being committed (for an arrest) or that evidence/contraband of a crime will be found in a particular place (for a search).

  • Search Warrant (#6): Always needs Probable Cause (supported by oath or affirmation).
  • Building PC (#18): One thing can't make probable cause, but multiple things can (individual innocent or minor observations combine under the totality of the circumstances).
  • Nature of PC (#23): Probable cause is NOT a standardized system — it is a fluid, practical, common-sense concept, not a rigid formula or fixed percentage.

Standing, Exclusionary Rule & Discovery

Items #9, #10, #13
Standing (#9)

Having a personal stake in something (a personal Reasonable Expectation of Privacy in the area searched or property seized) required to challenge a search or seizure in court.

Exclusionary Rule (#10)

The rule that applies when something (illegally obtained evidence) gets thrown out of court because it was seized in violation of the constitution.

Discovery Process (#13)

The pre-trial process that can handle all documents relevant to your case (reports, field notes, supplements, recordings shared with the defense/court).

Part 3

Seizures vs. Arrests, Terry Detentions, 4 Elements of Arrest & Deadly Force

Seizures vs. Arrests & The 4 Elements of Arrest

Items #1, #2, #15
  • Is every seizure an arrest? (#1): NO! Every arrest is a 4th Amendment seizure, but not every seizure is an arrest (for example, a Terry investigative stop is a temporary seizure, not an arrest).
  • What is an arrest under the Constitution? (#2): An arrest is a seizure under the 4th Amendment (requiring Probable Cause).

The 4 Elements of an Arrest (#15)

  1. Intention to arrest
  2. Communication (of that intent to the subject)
  3. Real or pretend authority
  4. Detention (actual physical restraint or voluntary submission to custody)

Investigative Detention (Terry Stop) Rules

Items #7, #14, #27
  • RAS to Detain vs. Frisk (#14): If you have Reasonable Articulable Suspicion (RAS) that criminal activity is afoot, you may detain them — NOT frisk them! (A frisk requires separate, additional RAS that the subject is armed and dangerous.)
  • Specific Crime Not Required (#27): For an investigative detention stop, you do NOT need to know the specific crime — only reasonable articulable suspicion that general criminal activity is afoot.
  • Handcuffs & Seating During Detention (#7): You CAN place a subject in handcuffs and/or seated if they are detained (when reasonably necessary for officer safety or to prevent flight) without turning the detention into an arrest.

Felony Arrest + Search Incident to Arrest & Deadly Force on Fleeing Subject

Items #17 & #20
Deadly Force Upon a Fleeing Subject (#17)

To use deadly force upon a fleeing subject, Probable Cause is needed (specifically, Probable Cause to believe the suspect poses a significant threat of death or serious physical injury to the officer or others — Tennessee v. Garner).

Subject Points a Gun at Your Head (#20)

Pointing a gun at your head is a felony (1st Degree Assault) — arrest and Search Incident to Arrest (SILA) of the person and their wingspan/immediate control area.

Part 4

Home Exigencies, Domestic Abuse, Stalking & "Evidence Or" Rules

3 Exigencies for Entering a House & "Evidence Or"

Items #4 & #16

3 Exigencies Allowing Warrantless Entry into a House (#4)

  1. Hot / fresh pursuit
  2. Protection of police or others (emergency aid / life safety)
  3. Destruction of evidence (preventing imminent destruction/removal)
"Evidence Or" — Returning the Evidence (#16)

Can't lock them up if they give you the evidence back ("Evidence Or"). Under Maryland's conditional warrantless misdemeanor arrest rules, if the justification to arrest is to prevent the suspect from tampering with, disposing of, or destroying evidence, and the suspect already gave you the evidence back (with no other arrest exigency present), you cannot lock them up without a warrant.

Warrantless Arrest for Domestic Abuse (vs. Child Abuse)

Items #21 & #26

Requirements for Domestic Abuse Warrantless Arrest (#26)

  1. Report made within 48 hours of the incident
  2. Parties are married (spouses) OR living together (cohabiting)
  3. There is visible physical injury (evidence of physical injury)
  4. Exigency exists (may not be apprehended, may cause injury/property damage, or may tamper with/destroy evidence)
Crucial Exception (#21)

Child abuse is NOT a domestic! The Maryland domestic abuse warrantless arrest statute (CP § 2-204) applies only to spouses or cohabitants — it does not classify child abuse as a domestic abuse arrest.

3 Criteria of Stalking Needed to Make a Warrantless Arrest (MD CP § 2-205)

Item #19

Under Maryland Criminal Procedure § 2-205, an officer may arrest a person without a warrant for Stalking only if all 3 statutory criteria are met:

1. Probable Cause

The officer has probable cause to believe the person has engaged in stalking (CR § 3-802).

2. Independent Credible Evidence

There is credible evidence OTHER than the statements of the alleged stalking victim to support the probable cause.

3. Imminent Danger OR Flight Risk

The officer has reason to believe the victim (or another person) is in danger of imminent bodily harm OR the stalker will not be apprehended unless immediately arrested.
